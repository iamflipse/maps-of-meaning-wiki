# 50. Lifted Up Like the Serpent: You Become Immune to What You Attend To

## What Peterson says (paraphrase)

The Passion multiplies every dimension of tragedy: young, torturous, deliberately exemplary death, brought about by friends, community, religious authorities and tyrants, in front of his mother, in disgrace. Christ says he must be lifted up as Moses lifted the serpent, linking himself to a story thousands of years older about healing through exposure. The key realization: you become immune to what you attend to. The crucifix replaces the bronze serpent as a deeper exposure, to malevolent suffering itself, and that image of disgraceful death, the inverse of a glorified emperor, became the center of churches and towns. Peterson argues the principle of voluntary self-sacrifice became the foundation of the state, and that this is why Christian-majority countries outside Africa thrive. In John, the Word is the logos; the hero unites the law and the prophets, tradition and vision. He says he wrote each sentence of Maps of Meaning many times to understand malevolence.

## Key ideas (paraphrase)

- The Passion is the sum of all horrors, a limit case.
- You become immune to what you attend to.
- Look at what is most poisonous with eyes guided by faith in the good.
- Voluntary self-sacrifice became the center of Western communities.
- The hero unites tradition (the law) and vision (the prophets).
