# 7. Anomaly: Dragon and Treasure

**Part 2: Order, Chaos and the Unknown** · transcript words 13,325–15,489 (2,165 words)

← [6. Setting the Bar and the Unexpected Event](06-setting-the-bar.md) · [8. Stress, Nested Games, and Yin and Yang](08-stress-yin-yang.md) →

## What Peterson says (paraphrase)

Obstacles and novelty both produce negative emotion, in proportion to how many of the game's rules they violate. An anomaly is an agent of magical transformation: a dragon and a treasure at once. Small novelty draws curiosity; larger novelty freezes you first, then exploration takes over. The novel is everything your game left out, like the serpent in the garden. That is why children should practice losing, why entrepreneurs often fail before they succeed, and why voluntary exposure in behavior therapy makes people braver. Every object carries a range of possibility, like a beer bottle that becomes a knife. How to face the radically unknown is, for Peterson, the religious meta-skill, which Genesis frames as truth aimed at love. He tells of a security guard who disarmed a robber and bought him a meal, and introduces the mythological landscape and the romantic adventure.

## Key ideas (paraphrase)

- Novelty produces negative emotion in proportion to how much it disrupts your game.
- An anomaly is both threat and promise: dragon and treasure.
- No gardens without serpents: what you ignore still exists.
- Voluntary exposure does not reduce fear; it makes you braver.
- Facing the unknown well is the most important meta-skill.
