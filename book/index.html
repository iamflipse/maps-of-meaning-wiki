Maps of Meaning: Two Ways of Looking at the World

A plain-language study guide to Jordan B. Peterson's Maps of Meaning lecture series, "Two Ways of Looking at the World": 54 sections in 11 parts, 40 concepts, and short quotes checked word for word against the transcript.

Start with the Cheat Sheet: the core idea, seven main points, six practices, and a one-minute version.

What this is

A study wiki. Peterson's lectures run about 99,000 words with no headings. Here they are split into 54 sections in lecture order, grouped into 11 parts, with titles added by this wiki. Each section page has a summary marked as paraphrase, key ideas, two to four short quotes, questions to explore and related links. The lecture transcript itself is not published.

How to use it

  • Read in order. The sidebar follows the lectures from the first claim (there are two ways of looking at the world) to the closing questions on humility.
  • Read by idea. Concepts collects 40 recurring ideas (aim, chaos and order, conscience, sacrifice, the hero, the brazen serpent and more), each with the sections where it comes up.
  • Search by modern words. Themes maps common search words to the concepts and sections that cover them.
  • Check the sources. The coverage map gives the transcript word range of every section. About explains how the wiki was made.

Use the search icon (top left) or press S to search the whole book.

Peterson's argument in one paragraph (paraphrase)

Peterson argues that the world can be seen as a place of objects or as a forum for action, and that the second, the world of value and story, is primary for living. We perceive through aims; emotion tracks progress toward them; life is a set of nested games whose ethic matters more than any single win. Order is explored territory and chaos is the unknown, and the stories that endure share a meta-story of descent into chaos and renewal, populated by archetypes such as the great mother, the great father and the hero. He reads Genesis, Cain and Abel, Jacob, Exodus, Job, Jonah, Elijah, the brazen serpent and the Passion, along with Mesopotamian and Egyptian myth, as accounts of how to face the unknown and malevolence. Recurring themes are aiming upward, listening to conscience, telling the truth, voluntary sacrifice, and voluntarily confronting what you fear, which he connects to exposure therapy. The series closes with images of the tree of life, a call to take on responsibility, and a case for humility.
